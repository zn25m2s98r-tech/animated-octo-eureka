What You're About to Do

A crash course in coding content — the quantitative kind

In this activity, you'll work through the entire content analysis process, from learning a coding scheme to applying it to real-ish headlines and comparing your results.

The Scenario

A research team wants to study how local news outlets frame stories about crime. They've sampled 8 recent headlines and opening sentences. Your job is to be a coder — to read each item and classify it using a standardized coding scheme.

The Point

Content analysis only works if different coders apply the scheme consistently. After you code the headlines, you'll compare your results to a "second coder" to see how well you agree. That comparison is called intercoder reliability — and it's the thing that separates systematic research from "I read some articles and had opinions."

The Coding Scheme

Read this carefully — you'll be applying it in a moment

For each headline, you'll code four variables. Every variable has predefined categories. Your job is to pick the one that best fits. There are no trick questions — but some will require judgment, which is exactly the point.

VariableCategoriesHow to Decide
Framing Law Enforcement Community/Victim Neutral/Factual Whose perspective dominates? Who's the main actor — police/courts, or community members/victims? If neither, it's neutral.
Tone Alarming Sympathetic Matter-of-Fact Does the language create urgency/fear, evoke empathy, or simply report facts?
Crime Type Focus Violent Property Drug/Vice Other/General What category of crime is the story primarily about?
Mentions Race/Ethnicity Yes No Does the text explicitly reference the race or ethnicity of anyone involved?
Important: Code only what's in the text. Don't infer, don't read between the lines, and don't bring in outside knowledge. If the headline doesn't mention race, code it as "No" — even if you know the real case involved a racial dimension. You're coding the content, not the reality.

Intercoder Reliability

The moment of truth — did you and Coder B agree?

Why This Matters

If two people can't apply the same coding scheme and get the same results, your data is meaningless. Intercoder reliability is the check that makes content analysis research instead of opinion.

In real research: Researchers typically aim for at least 80% agreement (or a Cohen's kappa of 0.80+) before they trust the data. When agreement is low, it usually means the coding scheme needs to be clearer — not that the coders are bad. Ambiguity in the scheme is the #1 cause of disagreement.

What You Just Did

And why it matters for your research

1. You applied a coding scheme to real content

You took unstructured text and classified it into predetermined categories. That's the core skill of content analysis.

2. You produced quantitative data

Your codes became frequencies that can be analyzed statistically — percentages, comparisons, tests of significance. Qualitative content became quantitative data.

3. You experienced intercoder reliability firsthand

Where you and Coder B disagreed, the coding scheme was probably ambiguous. In real research, you'd revise the scheme and retrain until agreement is consistently high.

4. You saw the trade-off

Some of these headlines were genuinely hard to code. The nuance you felt — the "it depends" instinct — is exactly what gets lost when you turn text into numbers. That's the price of systematicity. Whether it's worth it depends on your research question.

For your final project: If you're proposing content analysis as your method, your methods section needs to describe your coding scheme in this level of detail — what variables you're coding, what the categories are, and how you'd establish intercoder reliability.
